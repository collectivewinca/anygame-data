Do plain-looking sim games really sell better? We checked 10 stores — November 2026 edition

November 2026 edition · data scraped Nov 27, 2026

Previous edition: October 2026 storefront briefs

A viral post says indie developers should make simple-looking management, factory, colony and idle games instead of pretty ones. We re-scraped all ten stores we track and tested each claim against the numbers.

Bottom line · The claims · 10 stores · Timeline · Shortlist · Gaps · Method & runs · Caveats

Bottom line

Partly true, mostly on Steam, and not the way the post says. On Steam, sims do a bit better than the average paid game: 31% reach 100+ reviews, against 25% for all paid games. Colony, city-builder and factory games with 100+ reviews have some of the highest estimated sales of any genre. Idle and clicker games are the opposite: they rank last of 206 Steam tags.

The idea that players forgive plain art if they play for hours can't be tested, because no store we scrape publishes playtime. Ratings point the other way: sims are less often rated 85%+ positive than other games (41% vs 48% on Steam).

Outside Steam the advantage mostly disappears. Sims trail other games on PlayStation and Google Play and are level on GOG and Xbox. Roblox is the exception in volume: tycoon and simulator experiences are 30% of what we found there. They get better like-ratios than other experiences, but fewer visits.

For AnyGame: the useful part of the post is the method: find a genre where players stay a long time, then test a rough prototype for playtime before spending money. Do that with colony, city and factory games, not idle games. Expect the hard part to be the fun loop, as the replies said, not the art.

Where the claim comes from

On Nov 27, 2026 (9:17 AM ET), @froessell posted screenshots of an answer from Google's Gemini. It said to build "spreadsheet with graphics" sims (factory/automation, colony sim, management/tycoon, idle/incremental) on one reused engine across 2–3 games. The answer cites no sources. Its revenue numbers match published estimates: Chris Zukowski's analysis of VG Insights data for 2019 Steam releases, which found a median of about $1,136 lifetime and 68% under $10,000, and the "$1,100–$3,500" range on Steam Page Analyzer. The replies mostly agreed, with one caveat: balance and fun are still the hard part, and playtime is the number that matters.

Timeline: how the sim category grew

Release years come from each store's listed release date (Roblox: the date the experience was created). These catalogs only include games still listed today, so older years undercount games that were removed. Our Steam catalog is SteamSpy's app list (~83k apps), which lags new releases, so 2025–2026 cover only part of what Steam actually released.

On Steam the sim share has roughly tripled: 3–5% of each year's releases from 2012 to 2019, 10% in 2021–2023, 11% in 2024 and 14% in 2025. In every year, the median sim got more reviews than the median other game (for example 106 vs 24 for 2018 releases and 23 vs 14 for 2023). The jump came in 2020–2021, after Factorio's 1.0 (Aug 2020) and Dyson Sphere Program (Jan 2021).

What this means for AnyGame

  • Use the method, not the numbers. Pick genres with long play sessions and loyal players (colony, city, factory). Build a one-screen prototype and measure how long people play before spending on art or the $100 Steam fee.
  • Avoid idle and clicker games on Steam. They rank last for estimated sales, and on mobile and Roblox they mostly win through free-to-play money-making, not $15–25 sales.
  • Ship on PC first. On consoles (PlayStation, Xbox, Nintendo), sims are about 1% of catalogs and don't do better.
  • Fun and balance are the risk, not art. Sims are less often rated 85%+ positive, so players are hard on systems that don't work. Plan time for playtesting and balance.
  • Next step if you want it: measure real playtime for the shortlist from public sources (SteamSpy's playtime estimates or HowLongToBeat) and test the 10h+ claim directly.

Method and runs

Data (all scraped Nov 27, 2026 with the same actors and inputs as October). Steam: collectivewin/steam-catalog-scraper 0.1.3, same input as the October run FwiVYa7CgChoV7Tpc. The first same-input run (9bBfwOliPCfx0mQeK, dataset w2KKxCu9X9J1mXpkV) stopped early because SteamSpy's app list kept failing ("Too many connections"). We then ran 24 runs of the same actor and build, each covering one page or chunk of the list. Where SteamSpy's list failed, we gave the run the October app list plus 45 freshly fetched SteamSpy pages and used the actor's own resume step, so it only enriched apps. Steam's store API failed for 8,709 apps; for those we kept fresh reviews and tags and took store fields (price, date, publisher, art) from October. Result: 83,468 apps. All run and dataset IDs are in refresh/runs-steam-2026-11-27.json.

The other nine stores used their October production batches, re-run with identical input, build and memory. Their run and dataset IDs are listed under each store above and in refresh/runs-other-stores-2026-11-27.json. Each store was normalized with its own tools/<store>/merge.py and derive_tags.py.

Revenue estimate (Steam only): reviews × copies-per-review × list price. We use 20, 30 and 60 copies per review, the range GameDiscoverCo (Simon Carless) reported for post-2020 Steam games (Aug 2020 and Nov 14, 2022 surveys; 2022 median about 29, and about 59 for games selling 50–100k copies). This is gross before Steam's 30% cut, refunds, taxes and discounts. It is an estimate, not sales data.

Gap engine: the same method as the October Steam run (tag triples, bge-small-en-v1.5, lowest nearest-neighbor similarity), limited to triples that include a sim tag. 75,021 of 83,379 game embeddings were reused from October because their text hadn't changed; the other 8,358 were re-embedded.

Caveats

  • Gross revenue is estimated from reviews, never measured. Real multipliers vary from about 15 to 100+ copies per review.
  • Steam's 2025–2026 coverage is incomplete: SteamSpy's list lags new releases.
  • We scraped during Steam's autumn sale. Prices use the higher of the October and November values.
  • Steam's "Incremental" tag appears in our November data (236 games) but not in October's. We count it as idle.
  • PlayStation's first US batch failed (out of memory, as in October) after 400 rows. The next batch covered the rest of the US store, so coverage is similar to October. One GOG and one PlayStation country batch found nothing new.
  • Google Play's October Educational top-up (77 games, added locally) was not repeated.
  • Keyword-based tags (consoles, Epic, App Store) miss many sims and mislabel some. We dropped launchers (Roblox, Fortnite) from the top-game lists.
  • No store gives us playtime, so the central "playtime forgives ugliness" claim is untested.
